## Script (voice text)
In 1919, a wave of molasses twenty-five feet high ran through the streets of Boston.
It's January in the North End. After days of bitter cold, the day is suddenly warm, and the streets are busy.
Above them stands a steel tank, fifty feet tall, holding two point three million gallons of molasses.
It leaks so badly that kids fill pails from the drips.
When neighbours complained, the company painted the tank brown, so the leaks wouldn't show.
Then a ship tops it up with warm molasses, and the tank starts to groan.
At lunchtime, the rivets pop like gunfire, and the walls split.
The wave rolls down Commercial Street at thirty-five miles an hour, knocks a firehouse off its foundation, and slams into the elevated railway.
Luckily, the wave slowed down within minutes.
Unluckily, that's when it got worse.
Molasses is like honey. Warm, it pours. Straight from the fridge, it barely moves.
In the winter air, the warm molasses cooled and thickened, and trapped the people it caught.
Twenty-one people died. About a hundred and fifty were hurt.
The courts blamed the company, and Boston began making engineers sign and stamp their plans, a rule that spread across the country.
Crews hosed the streets with harbour water for weeks.
And for decades, on hot summer days, the North End still smelled of molasses.
